# Visual components

Read this guide and open `assets/source-preview.jpg` whenever a document needs a graph, percentage indicator, numbered explainer, callout or quotation. Follow the original [Gradient annual report cover template](https://www.magnific.com/free-vector/gradient-annual-report-cover-template_2446649.htm) while retaining Craig's approved Word typography and layout. Use components only when they help explain the content; they are available patterns, not a requirement to add graphics to every document.

## Shared visual system

Use white page backgrounds, the retained pink-to-purple gradient, deep purple headings and generous whitespace. Reuse or sample the retained artwork to match its colours; use the approved heading purple #71218A consistently. Keep Montserrat throughout: Black for major titles and prominent figures, SemiBold for labels and H2s, Regular for explanatory copy. Avoid default chart fonts and generic blue chart palettes. Small supporting labels may use 9–10 pt type where legible at final size; callout body text should normally remain 10.5–11 pt.

The Word master controls margins, headings, footer and metadata. The original reference controls the graphic vocabulary; it does not override approved dates or restore the original tiny placeholder type. A graph, panel or quote card may occupy the full text width or share a balanced row when its text remains readable. Do not squeeze several dense panels into a page to imitate the six-page overview image.

## Charts

For a column chart, use bars with a vertical pink-at-bottom to purple-at-top gradient, simple grey axes, a white plot area and minimal grid lines. Direct value labels may use compact charcoal badges with white Montserrat text above the bars, as in the reference. Reserve enough space above the tallest bar for its badge. Use readable sentence case category labels rather than the original miniature all-caps placeholders.

Carry the palette and typography into other appropriate chart types: purple and pink series for lines, areas or comparisons, with distinguishable markers or patterns when needed. Keep identical units and scales across comparable panels. Choose the chart type from the data and the reader's question, rather than converting every dataset into bars or a doughnut.

Use verified input values with visible units, category labels and a source/date where relevant. Bar charts start at zero; percentage charts use a truthful percentage scale; line-chart ranges may vary when clearly labelled. Do not invent values, treat missing data as zero, distort proportions, add 3D perspective or use colour alone to identify a critical series. Keep supporting data available when the recipient needs to check or edit a chart.

## Percentage rings

Use a light grey remainder, a pink-to-purple progress arc and a white centre with a prominent purple percentage. Start the arc at 12 o'clock and size it from the actual value. A 0% value has no coloured arc, and 100% has no grey remainder. Pair the ring with a concise label and a short explanation beside it; do not rely on a number without its meaning or denominator.

Use this pattern only for a genuine share or progress against a defined total. Do not force counts, negative values, values above 100% or unrelated categories into progress rings. Subtle source-inspired depth is optional; it must not change the apparent value or reduce legibility.

## Numbered explainers

Use a short vertical sequence of thin purple or gradient-outlined circles with dark Montserrat Black numerals such as 01 and 02, paired with aligned explanatory paragraphs. Keep equal circle diameters, consistent gaps and readable prose. These are suitable for a real sequence or a small set of key points; use ordinary prose for long explanations.

## Callouts and panels

For a prominent goals, finding or action panel, use a rectangular pink-to-purple gradient, a short bold white heading and white explanatory text with generous padding. For quieter supporting notes, retain the approved pale purple callout treatment. Choose prominence from the importance of the content, rather than applying the saturated treatment to every note.

Keep body text left aligned and concise, with padding normally around 6–8 mm. Adjust the gradient to a darker magenta/purple where needed for readable white text. Use a sidebar only when the remaining main text column is still comfortable to read. For paired panels such as vision and mission, align their top edges, headings and padding; allow sufficient height for the real content rather than clipping or shrinking it.

## Quote cards

Use a solid pink, magenta or purple rectangle with white Montserrat text and source-inspired white quotation marks in opposite corners. Keep generous padding, with clear space between the quote, its attribution and the quotation marks. Two cards may share a row if both remain readable; additional cards can continue naturally onto another page.

Use quotation marks only for a genuine sourced quotation and retain the speaker or source attribution. Never turn a paraphrase or an invented example into a quote. An ordinary key-message card uses a heading or statement without decorative quotation marks. Centre only a short pull quote; longer quoted passages should be left aligned or remain normal document text.

## Production and verification

Generate charts deterministically from their data using plotting or vector tools, not image generation. Prefer native Word charts or editable/vector artwork where the export workflow supports them reliably. If a graphic must be rasterised for Word, render it at least 300 dpi at its intended printed size and keep document text outside the graphic selectable. Use editable Word text for callouts and captions where practical. Never rasterise an entire document page to reproduce a layout.

Render the final Word/PDF pages and inspect each component at its delivered size. Check font embedding, labels, units, values, ring proportions, text contrast, alignment and page breaks. Compare the result against the original overview and the approved Word master, correcting mismatched colours, substituted fonts, cramped labels or clipped content before delivery.
